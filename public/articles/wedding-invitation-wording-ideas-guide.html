Wedding Invitation Wording & Design Ideas

Seven design ideas and wording examples to help you make the invitation your own.

The short version

  • Put the practical details first: names, date, start time, venue and RSVP information. A beautiful card still has to tell people where to go.
  • Choose one visual idea and carry it through the type, paper and envelope. Seven options below cover understated, illustrated, tactile and digital approaches.
  • Use the wording examples as starting points. Say clearly whether an invitation is for the full day or the evening reception only.
  • Ask someone outside the planning group to proof a printed sample before ordering the full batch.

Start with the details guests need

A wedding invitation has two jobs. It sets the tone for the day, and it gives your guests enough information to turn up at the right place and time. Settle the second job before you choose a typeface or a paper finish.

Write down the couple’s names, the day and full date, the ceremony start time, the venue name and address, and the reply deadline. If the reception is elsewhere, give its address too. State exactly who is invited: a named partner, a whole family, or one guest. For an evening invitation, lead with the evening reception and its own start time.

Travel, parking, accommodation, a dress code and a gift list can go on an information card or wedding website. Keep the essentials on the invitation itself, especially if some guests will not use a QR code. Our wedding stationery guide covers the wider suite and sending timeline.

Seven invitation design ideas

Pick a direction that suits the venue and the day you are planning. A small detail that belongs to your wedding usually feels more personal than adding several fashionable finishes at once.

1. Let the typography do the work

Use a warm white or ivory card, one expressive typeface for the names and one easy-to-read typeface for the details. Give the date and location space to breathe. This works particularly well for a modern city wedding or a small restaurant reception. Test the text at its actual printed size: fine lettering that looks elegant on a laptop can disappear on paper.

For a truly minimalist invitation, compare three versions before ordering: names centred above the details; names aligned left with generous margins; and a two-column layout with the date set apart. Choose the one your guest can understand quickest. If you want a monogram, put it on the back or envelope flap so it does not compete with the venue and time.

2. Use a restrained rustic detail

For a barn or countryside venue, choose textured paper and a single botanical drawing, a blind-embossed motif or a simple envelope liner. Avoid stacking kraft card, twine, wax seals and several illustrations unless that genuinely reflects the day. A restrained palette helps the information stay clear.

A location-specific illustration can replace flowers altogether: think of a Welsh chapel, a mountain outline or a line of trees on the drive to the venue. If the drawing is detailed, keep the wording panel plain. Ask for a version that also works on the order of service, so the idea connects the day without requiring every item to look identical.

3. Borrow a colour from the season

A spring invitation might use soft green with cream; an autumn card could pair rust with deep brown. Keep the colours legible and check a physical proof, since screens and print stocks render them differently. For a winter wedding, a single metallic accent can do more than a full foil border.

Colour can also distinguish the suite: one shade on the main card, a quieter tint on the RSVP card and a contrasting envelope. Check how those colours look in daylight and warm indoor light. If the invitation has a photograph or a painted background, put the practical details on a solid, high-contrast area rather than over the image.

4. Make the place the artwork

Commission a line drawing of the venue, use a licensed photograph you took there, or sketch a map of a meaningful street. A recognisable place is more personal than a generic wedding graphic. Confirm that you have permission to use any artwork before sending it to print.

Another approach is to draw the route between the ceremony and reception. Keep it decorative, then give the complete addresses in type. A hand-drawn map should help guests recognise the place; it should never be their only source of directions.

5. Add one tactile finish

Letterpress, a deckled edge, embossed initials or a coloured envelope can make a simple card memorable. Ask your printer for one sample of the exact paper and finish. Weight and thickness affect postage and how easily the invitation fits in its envelope, so test the complete suite rather than the card alone.

Get quotes for a standard card first, then price the finish as an extra. If a technique makes the text harder to read or pushes the suite into a more expensive postage band, keep it for the cover or envelope. You can make a strong impression with good paper and a clean print without a luxury process on every insert.

6. Pair print with a useful digital layer

A printed invitation can carry a short URL or QR code leading to travel information and an RSVP form. Test the code on both iPhone and Android, and keep a written URL beside it. Do not hide the date, time or venue behind the link. This gives guests a physical keepsake while letting you update practical information if plans change.

For an entirely digital invitation, use a simple layout that opens well on a phone. Put the RSVP button near the date and venue, make the link shareable only with the intended guests where your system allows it, and confirm that guests can correct a mistaken reply. Offer a phone or email reply route for anyone who does not want to use a form.

7. Use an itinerary for a destination wedding

If guests are travelling, show the main wedding date prominently and put other events on a separate information card or website. Label welcome drinks and brunch as optional where appropriate. Include the venue address and local time zone; avoid printing tentative arrangements that might change. A small route map can be useful when venues are far apart.

If the trip includes more than one day, separate confirmed plans from optional suggestions. “Wedding ceremony: Saturday at 4.00 pm” is much clearer than printing a packed timetable when brunch and excursions are still being arranged. Guests can then book travel around the event they are actually invited to attend.

Wedding invitation wording examples

Good wedding invitation wording sounds like the people hosting the day. The examples below are original templates, so replace every name, place and date before sending. You can use the same wedding invitation text on a printed card or in a digital invitation.

Simple and modern: full day

Alex Morgan and Sam Patel
invite you to celebrate their wedding
on Saturday 12 June 2027 at 2.00 pm
at The Assembly Rooms, Bath.
Dinner and dancing will follow.
Please reply by 1 May at alexandsam.example.

A short wedding invitation sentence like this is direct, warm and easy to scan. Add the full venue address on the reverse or an information card if space is tight.

Formal wording with parents as hosts

Mr and Mrs Morgan and Mr and Mrs Patel
request the pleasure of your company
at the marriage of Alex Morgan and Sam Patel
on Saturday 12 June 2027 at two o’clock in the afternoon
at The Assembly Rooms, Bath.
A reception will follow.
Kindly reply by 1 May 2027.

Name the actual hosts rather than following a rule about whose parents must appear first. If several people are hosting, “Together with their families” is a neat alternative.

Couple hosting with their families

Together with their families,
Alex and Sam would love you to join them
for their wedding on Saturday 12 June 2027.
The ceremony begins at 2.00 pm at The Assembly Rooms, Bath,
followed by a reception at the same venue.
Please RSVP by 1 May.

Evening reception only

Alex and Sam invite you to celebrate their wedding
at an evening reception
on Saturday 12 June 2027 from 7.00 pm
at The Assembly Rooms, Bath.
Drinks and dancing to follow.
Please reply by 1 May.

The phrase “evening reception” makes the scope clear. Avoid saying “join us for our wedding” on an evening card if the ceremony and meal are not included.

Casual invitation

We’re getting married!
Come celebrate with us on Saturday 12 June 2027
at The Assembly Rooms, Bath.
Ceremony at 2.00 pm, then food, drinks and dancing.
Let us know by 1 May if you can make it.

Invitation phrases for wedding cards work best when the practical details remain explicit. A playful first line should never replace the time or venue.

Destination wedding

Alex and Sam are getting married in Lisbon
on Saturday 12 June 2027 at 4.00 pm local time.
Join us at [venue name and full address].
Reception to follow.
Travel notes and RSVP: [short URL].
Please reply by [date].

Give guests plenty of notice before asking them to commit to travel. Only include booked venues and confirmed times on the card.

Celebration after a private ceremony

We tied the knot in a small ceremony.
Now we would love to celebrate with you.
Join Alex and Sam on Saturday 12 June 2027
from 7.00 pm at The Assembly Rooms, Bath.
Please let us know by 1 May if you can come.

Use this only when the ceremony has already happened or will be private. It sets the right expectation without asking guests to guess whether a formal ceremony is part of their invitation.

Civil partnership celebration

Alex and Sam invite you to celebrate their civil partnership
on Saturday 12 June 2027 at 2.00 pm
at The Assembly Rooms, Bath.
A reception will follow at the same venue.
Please RSVP by 1 May.

Use the words that describe your ceremony and relationship. There is no need to force marriage wording onto a civil partnership invitation.

Children and plus-ones

Address the card to the people invited. If you need a line on the information card, keep it kind and specific: “We can only accommodate the guests named on the invitation.” For a child-free celebration, “We’re looking forward to an adults-only celebration” is clear. Where an exception applies, speak privately to the affected family rather than leaving them to interpret a general note.

If you are looking for wedding card wordings, start with a short line you can adapt rather than a rigid formula. The most useful version is the one that gives the guest certainty: who, when, where and how to reply.

Build your invitation text, line by line

If none of the examples above fits, write the invitation in six short parts. This is easier than trying to polish a single long sentence. It also makes the design more flexible: a printer can give each part its own line without breaking the meaning.

1. Who is inviting the guest?

Begin with the names of the people hosting, or simply the couple’s names. “Alex and Sam invite you” is enough if the couple are organising the day. “Together with their families” acknowledges family support without implying that a particular person is paying. If parents or relatives want to be named, agree the order and spelling with them before the proof goes to print.

There is no rule that the bride’s parents must host a modern UK wedding invitation. Families come in many shapes; use names that reflect yours. If parents are separated or remarried, list only the people who are actually extending the invitation. It is also perfectly fine to leave hosts out and begin with “Please join us”.

2. What are guests being invited to?

Say “the marriage of”, “their wedding”, “their civil partnership” or “an evening reception” as appropriate. The distinction matters. An evening guest needs to know when to arrive without reading between the lines. If the ceremony is private and the invitation is only for a later celebration, state that plainly rather than using a generic ceremony invitation.

3. When is it?

Give the day of the week, date, year and start time. Writing “Saturday 12 June 2027 at 2.00 pm” is compact and hard to misread. On a very formal card you can spell out the time, but clarity still comes first. If there is an arrival window or a separate reception time, put it on its own line. Avoid “from 2 pm” if the ceremony itself begins at 2 pm and guests should be seated earlier.

4. Where should they go?

Use the venue’s full name and an address or postcode. If the ceremony and reception are at different locations, label each one. A venue with several buildings, entrances or similar names may need an extra note. Check the venue’s preferred arrival point and add a short direction on the information card if a satnav postcode leads to the wrong gate.

5. What follows?

“Reception to follow” is enough when both events are at the same place. If the ceremony is at a register office and dinner is across town, give the second venue and its start time. Do not promise a sit-down meal in the invitation unless that is what the guest is invited to. For a relaxed celebration, “Drinks and dancing to follow” may be a better description.

6. How do they reply?

Set a deadline and give one obvious reply route. That might be a reply card, email address, phone number or wedding website. If you offer more than one option, make sure replies end up on the same guest list. Ask for dietary requirements when guests respond, then confirm final numbers with the venue or caterer according to your booking terms.

Read the six parts together, aloud. If the invitation sounds stiff or the guest must search for the location, simplify it. A neat layout cannot rescue unclear wording.

Short phrases for RSVP and information cards

The main card does not need to carry every instruction. Move supporting details to a separate insert or wedding website, then write each note as you would say it to a guest. These examples can be adapted without changing the core wedding invitation wording.

RSVP

  • “Please reply by 1 May 2027 at [short URL]. Let us know about any dietary requirements when you respond.”
  • “Kindly return the enclosed reply card by 1 May 2027.”
  • “We have reserved two seats in your names. Please let us know if you can join us by 1 May.”

Use a date that leaves time for your actual supplier deadlines. If you have printed reply cards, include the return address and check whether you want to provide postage. For online RSVPs, test the form as a guest, including the “decline” route. A form that only lets people accept creates extra follow-up work.

Travel and accommodation

  • “The ceremony and reception are both at [venue]. The entrance for guests is on [street].”
  • “Parking is available at [location]. Please allow time to walk to the ceremony entrance.”
  • “We have listed nearby hotels and travel options at [short URL]. Please book directly with the provider.”

Do not describe accommodation as “reserved” if you have only collected suggestions. If there is a room allocation or a booking code, give the expiry date and explain whether guests pay the hotel directly. For a remote venue, include the final part of the journey rather than repeating information that maps already show.

Dress code and gifts

  • “Dress code: smart, comfortable and ready for the garden.”
  • “The ceremony will be outdoors if the weather allows; you may want shoes suitable for grass.”
  • “Your company is the gift. If you would still like to give something, details are on our website.”

A dress code is most helpful when it solves a practical question. “Garden party” may sound lovely but leave guests unsure whether the ground will be soft or the evening cold. Give the useful detail and keep the tone friendly. Any gift note should feel optional and can be left off the invitation entirely.

Guests with children

“We have reserved seats for the family members named on your invitation” is more precise than “children welcome” when capacity is limited. If the celebration is adults-only, say so on an insert and address invitations by name. Give close relatives with special arrangements a direct message so they are not left wondering whether an exception applies.

Choose the right tone

Start with the way you would invite a close friend in person. If “request the pleasure of your company” feels unlike you, write “would love you to join them”. If you want a formal card, keep the same level of formality throughout the invitation and RSVP instructions.

Be precise about the ceremony and reception. If the wedding breakfast is for a smaller group, send separate all-day and evening invitations, each with its own start time. List only named guests on the envelope or RSVP form. Do not rely on a dress code, a reception time or a family surname to convey who is included.

Gift list language can stay on a separate information card. “Your presence is enough; if you would like to give a gift, details are at [website]” is one option. If you would rather omit it altogether, that is fine too. Keep the invitation focused on welcoming people.

For Welsh or bilingual celebrations, check both language versions with a fluent speaker and allow space for different line lengths. Put the same essential information in both versions; the translation should not lose a venue detail or RSVP method.

For the RSVP card, try: “Please reply by 1 May 2027. We have reserved two seats in your names. Let us know about any dietary requirements when you respond.” Personalise the seat count for each household. This avoids a vague plus-one field and gives your caterer useful information in one reply.

If the ceremony and reception take place at separate venues, repeat the second address in a clear line: “Reception from 5.30 pm at [full address].” If there is a long gap, tell guests what to expect. If you are using a separate information card, label it plainly so it is not missed in the envelope.

Choose a format guests can actually use

A printed card, a digital invitation and a hybrid approach can all work. Choose based on your guests, the amount of information that might change, and the way you plan to collect replies. The best format is one that makes the next step obvious to the person receiving it.

Printed invitation

Print is useful when you want a keepsake or have guests who prefer a physical card. It makes the key details easy to find later on a fridge or noticeboard. The drawbacks are the time needed to proof, print, address and post each card, and the difficulty of correcting an address or time after delivery. Confirm the venue and schedule before approving print.

Use readable type and sufficient contrast. A pale grey script on cream paper may photograph well but frustrate a guest trying to read the time. Keep names and the ceremony details prominent. If the full address will not fit elegantly on the front, put it on the back or a clearly labelled insert instead of shrinking it.

Digital invitation

Digital cards are convenient for fast updates and online replies. Check the mobile layout before sending because many guests will open a message on their phone. Give the date, time, venue and RSVP method in the message itself, even if the full design lives on a website. That way a preview or forwarded link still carries the essential information.

Do not assume every guest uses the same messaging app or will receive a link from an unfamiliar sender. A short personal note makes the invitation easier to recognise. For guests who prefer a phone call or a printed card, provide one. Keep an accurate list of what was sent and where replies were recorded.

Hybrid invitation

A simple printed card with a short URL often gives the best of both approaches. Print the information that will not change, then use the website for travel notes, hotel details and RSVP updates. A QR code is a convenience, not the only route. If the page needs a password or individual invitation code, explain how guests can get help if it fails.

Whichever format you choose, consider guests with low vision or difficulty using small screens. A large-print version or a plain-text email costs little to prepare. Online information should have real text that can be enlarged and read by assistive technology, rather than a single image of the card.

Wording mistakes worth catching

Most invitation mistakes are small but consequential. A misplaced time, ambiguous guest name or broken reply link can create dozens of separate conversations. Look for these before the final approval:

  • Missing year: a beautiful “12 June” looks incomplete if invitations are sent far in advance or kept as a memento.
  • Unclear arrival time: say whether the printed time is when the ceremony begins or when guests should arrive.
  • Evening guests given the ceremony venue: put the evening reception venue and start time on their own version of the invitation.
  • Unnamed plus-ones: address people by name where possible. If an unnamed guest is invited, make that explicit on the envelope or RSVP form.
  • A QR code without a fallback: print a short URL or another way to reply.
  • A shortened venue name: include enough of the address for guests to distinguish similarly named locations.
  • Too many instructions on the main card: move hotel and parking notes to a separate information card while keeping the core event details visible.
  • Last-minute wording changes after design approval: ask for a fresh proof when names or timing change, as even one extra line can shift the layout.

If you have two guest groups, print a marked proof of each version. Have a friend read the all-day card and the evening card separately, without explaining the plan first. Their interpretation is a more useful test than another pass by someone who already knows every detail.

Proof and send

Before ordering, ask someone who has not seen the design to tell you the date, ceremony time, venue and RSVP deadline from the proof. If they hesitate, change the layout. Read every proper name aloud and check the postcode against the venue’s own details.

  • Check that all-day and evening versions have different, correct start times.
  • Confirm the spelling of names, venue, street, postcode and website URL.
  • Open every short link and QR code on a phone, then check the RSVP form works.
  • Print one sample at actual size and check contrast, folds and envelope fit.
  • Weigh the complete addressed suite and confirm the current postage with Royal Mail.
  • Keep a clean spare and record who has been sent which version.

Agree a final version with everyone named as a host before the printer receives it. A small wording change after approval can shift line breaks, which is why the last proof should show the exact paper size and layout. If you are using two invitation versions, review them side by side and make sure the correct guests receive each one.

Common questions

What should a wedding invitation say?

Include the couple's names, wedding date, ceremony time and venue, reception details if different, who is invited, and an RSVP deadline with a clear reply method.

How do you word an evening-only wedding invitation?

State the evening reception start time and venue plainly. Do not imply that the guest is invited to the ceremony or wedding breakfast.

Do parents have to be named as hosts?

No. Name the people who are inviting guests, whether that is the couple, their parents, both families, or another host. Choose wording that reflects your arrangements.

Can we put a QR code on a wedding invitation?

Yes. Keep the essential date, time and venue in print, then use a QR code for travel notes, accommodation and online RSVPs. Include a short written URL as a fallback.
