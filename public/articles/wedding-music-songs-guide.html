Wedding Music & Songs Guide

How to choose wedding ceremony music, pick a first dance song and brief your DJ or band for a reception playlist that suits every part of the day.

Ceremony Music

Ceremony music covers three distinct moments, and there's no rule that they all need to sound the same — many couples deliberately choose a different mood for each.

  • Processional: the song played as you walk down the aisle. Classical pieces are traditional, but a favourite song arranged for strings, or even the original recording, both work.
  • Signing the register: a quieter interlude, typically two to three minutes, while guests wait — a live musician or a pre-chosen playlist both work well here.
  • Recessional: an upbeat song as you leave the ceremony as a married couple — this is where couples most often pick something fun rather than formal.

If you're having a religious ceremony, check with the officiant or venue first — some venues restrict song choices to a pre-approved list, particularly for the processional.

Choosing Your First Dance

The first dance is one of the most photographed moments of the reception, but there's no fixed formula for what it has to sound or look like.

  • Full song or edited: many couples edit their chosen track down to 60–90 seconds rather than dancing to the full length — ask your DJ or band to prepare this in advance.
  • Meaningful vs. crowd-pleasing: a song with personal significance doesn't have to be a ballad — genre, tempo and lyrics are all up to you.
  • Mash-ups: a popular option that starts slow and builds into something upbeat, giving guests a natural cue to join in.
  • Dance lessons: entirely optional, but a few sessions can settle nerves even if you're not planning a choreographed routine.

Reception Playlist

Briefing a DJ or band well is what actually determines how the dance floor feels, more than the specific songs on a wishlist.

  • Genres and decades, not just songs: a list of eras and styles guides a good DJ better than a short list of specific tracks, and leaves room for reading the room.
  • A "do not play" list: just as useful as a wishlist — it heads off songs tied to an awkward memory or simply not to your taste.
  • Key moments: agree music for the entrance, background music during speeches, the cake cutting and the last song of the night.
  • Band or DJ: a live band brings energy but a smaller song range; a DJ covers more requests and genres. See EventFlow's wedding entertainment guide for how to choose between them.

Licensing & Practicalities

  • PRS and PPL: most UK venues already hold PRS for Music (composers) and PPL (recordings) licences covering any music played on-site, live or recorded — confirm this with the venue rather than assuming.
  • Noise curfews: many venues' licences set a cut-off time for live or amplified music, commonly around 11pm–midnight — check this before booking a band or DJ for a late finish.
  • Outdoor and unlicensed venues: if you're bringing your own sound system to a marquee, garden or other unlicensed space, check whether you need to arrange your own day licence. EventFlow's event licences guide covers the wider UK licensing basics.
  • Sound restrictions: rural or residential venues sometimes cap volume or require music to move indoors after a set time — ask about this at the booking stage, not the week of the wedding.

Common questions

How do I choose ceremony music?

Ceremony music splits into three moments that can each have a different song: the processional as you walk in, a quieter piece during the register signing, and an upbeat recessional as you leave. A string quartet, solo musician or a carefully chosen recording all work — check with your venue or registrar about any restrictions first.

Does my first dance song have to be a slow song?

No. A romantic ballad is traditional, but upbeat songs, genre mash-ups and even choreographed routines are all common choices now. Many couples also edit the track down to 60–90 seconds rather than dancing to the full song.

Do I need a music licence for my wedding?

Most UK wedding venues already hold PRS for Music and PPL licences covering live and recorded music played on their premises, but it's worth confirming rather than assuming — especially at an unlicensed or outdoor venue, or if you're bringing your own sound system.
