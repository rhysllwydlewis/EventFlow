Event Travel Costs: Budgeting Transport for Guests, Staff and Suppliers

Travel is one of the easiest event costs to underestimate. Here's how to budget properly for guests, staff, stakeholders and suppliers getting to and from your event — and where the savings actually are.

The short version

  • For events with meaningful organiser-funded transport, 5–10% can be a useful starting benchmark — not a universal rule.
  • Guest travel is an attendance problem before it's a cost problem — a venue that's awkward to reach shows up in your RSVP numbers.
  • Supplier travel can sit outside the headline day rate. Ask for the free-mileage radius before you compare quotes.
  • For employees using their own cars or vans, use HMRC's current approved rates as the tax benchmark and agree expenses in writing.
  • Price long or repeated journeys off real pump prices, not last quarter's estimate.

Why travel is the line that bites

Travel rarely gets its own line on an event budget — it gets absorbed into "miscellaneous" and then quietly becomes one of the biggest surprises on the final invoice. Parking, shuttle buses, taxis, staff mileage and supplier travel can become a material part of the total cost, particularly for remote venues, multi-site events or productions with repeated setup and breakdown journeys.

It's also one of the few costs that scales with decisions you make elsewhere in the plan. Choose a venue an hour further from the motorway and every supplier's callout fee can rise. Add a second site for photos or an after-party and you've added another transport leg. Getting travel right early avoids both budget shocks and, just as importantly, guests and crew who turn up stressed and late.

Getting guests to and from your event

Guest travel is about experience as much as cost. A venue that's difficult to reach — poor parking, no nearby station, unreliable evening buses — creates friction before anyone has even arrived, and it can affect attendance.

  • Parking: Confirm on-site capacity early. If it's limited, arrange overflow parking or a park-and-ride shuttle rather than leaving guests to find their own solution on the day.
  • Public transport links: Note the nearest station or stop, typical journey times, and last-service times in your invitations or event page — especially for evening finishes.
  • Shuttle buses: For venues off the beaten track, price a shared shuttle from a central point (station, hotel, car park) against the likely cost and complexity of individual taxis.
  • Accessibility: Factor in drop-off points, step-free routes and accessible parking bays for guests with mobility needs — this affects venue choice as much as budget.
  • Accommodation-linked travel: If guests are travelling long distances, compare a block-booked hotel with a short transfer against dispersed individual journeys on the night.

Put a "Getting there" section on your invitation, wedding website or event page. Journey options, parking notes and last-train times cut down guest questions and reduce late arrivals — and it costs you nothing but ten minutes of writing.

Staff, stakeholders, suppliers and agencies

This is the travel spend that catches organisers out, because it's spread across several different budget lines rather than one obvious "guest transport" heading.

Suppliers and crew

Photographers, caterers, AV crews and entertainers may price travel separately from their core fee — either as a flat callout charge or mileage above a free radius. Published supplier examples often use a radius around 20–30 miles, but policies vary considerably, so ask for the exact allowance and rate upfront. A cheaper day rate with a large travel surcharge can end up costing more than a supplier based closer to the venue.

Staff and volunteers

For corporate events, staff travelling between office and venue — or between multiple sites during a multi-day conference — need a clear mileage or expenses policy agreed in advance, not worked out after the event when receipts start arriving. Volunteer reimbursements can have different treatment, so document what will and will not be repaid.

Stakeholders and VIPs

Senior stakeholders, sponsors or speakers may warrant a more managed journey: a pre-booked taxi or a named parking space rather than being pointed to the overflow field. Budget for this separately so it doesn't get lost in the general transport line.

Agencies and multi-site activations

If you're running a touring activation, brand roadshow or multi-venue campaign, agency crews travelling with equipment between sites can become one of the larger transport costs. Build in fuel, van hire, congestion charges and overnight stays as a distinct budget block, and revisit it as the route firms up — early estimates based on a draft schedule are rarely accurate once real dates and distances are confirmed.

Calculating fuel and mileage costs

If you're reimbursing an employee who uses their own car or van for business travel, HMRC's approved mileage allowance for the 2026/27 tax year is 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles, then 25p per mile after that, effective from 6 April 2026. Payments up to the approved amount can normally be made without tax and National Insurance on the mileage element. Check HMRC's current approved mileage guidance before relying on the figure for another tax year. Freelancers and volunteers can have different expense and tax arrangements, so do not assume the employee mileage rules automatically apply to them.

Where a flat mileage rate doesn't fit — a supplier quoting for a long return trip, a van hire for an activation tour, or a shuttle contractor pricing fuel separately — estimate the journey from actual distance and a current pump price rather than an old rule-of-thumb figure. Fuel prices can move enough to make a stale estimate misleading. A live tracker such as fuelcosts.co.uk, which publishes UK forecourt pricing data, gives you a current figure to put into the calculator below.

Ways to reduce event travel costs

  • Book local first: Compare the complete quote, including travel, rather than assuming the lowest day rate is the cheapest overall.
  • Share the journey: Where it is safe and practical, grouping crew and equipment into fewer vehicles can simplify parking and reduce duplicated mileage.
  • Time it around traffic: If the venue and working hours allow it, scheduling setup or breakdown outside the busiest periods can reduce journey time and idling.
  • Negotiate a travel-inclusive rate: For suppliers making several visits (planning meetings, setup, event day), agree one transparent travel figure upfront instead of discovering separate charges later.
  • Compare shared transport with individual taxis: For a group travelling the same route, price a minibus or shuttle against the equivalent taxi plan rather than assuming either option is automatically cheaper.
  • Check the free-mileage radius: Some suppliers quote a free travel zone — confirm the exact mileage and what happens beyond it, since "local" means different things to different businesses.

Building travel into your event budget

Treat travel as its own budget category rather than folding it into venue or supplier costs — it makes it far easier to see where the money is actually going and to compare quotes fairly. As a starting point:

  1. List every group that needs to travel: guests, staff, suppliers, stakeholders and agency crew.
  2. Note distance, likely mode of transport and any surcharge or free-mileage radius for each.
  3. If routes, attendance or fuel prices are still uncertain, add a clearly labelled planning contingency. Around 10% can be a useful working allowance for some events, but set it from the actual uncertainty in your plan rather than treating 10% as a rule.
  4. Review actual costs after the event so next time's estimate starts from real numbers, not guesswork.

Common questions

Should you pay for guests' travel to an event?

For most weddings, parties and public events, guests cover their own travel. What organisers usually fund is the part guests can't arrange themselves — a shuttle from the station or hotel, overflow parking, or accessible drop-off. For corporate events where attendance is expected rather than optional, budget for travel and accommodation as part of the invitation.

What mileage rate should you pay event staff and volunteers?

For the 2026/27 tax year, HMRC's approved mileage allowance for employees using their own cars and vans is 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles and 25p per mile after that, effective from 6 April 2026. Volunteer and freelance arrangements can have different tax and expenses treatment, so agree those separately rather than assuming the employee rules apply.

Do event suppliers include travel in their quote?

Not consistently. Some suppliers include travel within a free radius — often around 20–30 miles in published examples, although policies vary considerably — and charge mileage or a flat callout fee beyond it. Others price travel separately for every visit. Ask for the free-mileage radius and the rate beyond it before you compare day rates.

How much of an event budget should go on transport?

There is no universal percentage. For events where organiser-funded transport is a meaningful cost, 5–10% can be a useful starting benchmark, but venue location, event format and who is paying for travel can move the figure substantially. Give travel its own budget category and use a clearly labelled contingency where the route or fuel cost is still uncertain.
