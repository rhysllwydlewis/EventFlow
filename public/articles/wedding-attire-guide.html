Wedding Attire Guide

What to wear to a wedding as a guest, bridesmaid or mother of the bride, plus how to choose a wedding dress.

The short version

  • Always check the invitation for a stated dress code first — it overrides any general "wedding guest" rule of thumb.
  • As a guest, avoid white/ivory (reserved for the bride) and be cautious with all-black at a religious or more traditional ceremony.
  • Choosing a wedding dress usually takes 6–9 months from first fitting to final alterations — start earlier for a fully bespoke gown.
  • "Who pays for the bridesmaid dress" has no fixed UK rule — some brides cover it, some bridesmaids do, and many split the cost. Agree it early to avoid an awkward conversation later.

Wedding Guest Dress Codes

Most UK wedding invitations state a dress code directly — check the invitation itself before guessing. When none is given, "wedding guest smart" is the safe general standard: smart, occasion-appropriate clothing, one notch more formal than you'd wear to a normal social event.

Black tie

Full evening formality: a tuxedo or dark suit with a bow tie for men, and a full-length formal gown or elegant cocktail dress for women. Black tie weddings are usually evening ceremonies or receptions at grander venues.

Formal / morning dress

A step down from black tie but still fully dressed up — suits (often morning dress for the wedding party) for men, and knee-length or longer dresses for guests. Common for daytime church or register office ceremonies.

Semi-formal / smart casual

The most common UK wedding dress code. A suit or smart trousers with a shirt for men, and a dress, smart jumpsuit or co-ord for women. Comfortable enough for an all-day event, still clearly "dressed for a wedding".

Casual / rustic

Increasingly common for barn, outdoor and festival-style weddings. Smart-casual clothing and comfortable shoes suited to grass, gravel or a marquee floor — still avoid jeans and trainers unless the invitation explicitly says so.

The golden rule for guests: avoid white, ivory or cream (reserved for the bride), think twice about all-black at a religious or more traditional ceremony, and check for any cultural or venue-specific guidance (bare shoulders, footwear on grass or a boat) on the invitation or wedding website.

Choosing a Wedding Dress

With so many silhouettes and price points, narrowing down a wedding dress usually comes down to three questions: what shape suits you, what the venue and season call for, and what your budget allows.

Common silhouettes

  • A-line: fitted through the bodice, flaring gradually to the hem — flattering on most body shapes and the most commonly chosen silhouette.
  • Ball gown: a fitted bodice with a dramatically full skirt — classic and formal, suited to larger, grander venues.
  • Sheath / column: a slim, fitted silhouette that follows the body's natural line — popular for beach, registry office or more minimalist weddings.
  • Mermaid / fishtail: fitted through the bodice and hips, flaring out below the knee — a striking, more formal option.
  • Tea-length or midi: a shorter hem for a more relaxed, vintage-leaning or registry-office look.

Practical timeline

Most UK bridal shops recommend ordering a dress 9–12 months before the wedding to allow for manufacturing and 2–3 rounds of alterations. A fully bespoke or designer gown can take longer; a sample-sale or off-the-peg dress can be ready in a matter of weeks if timescales are tight.

Budget-friendly options

Sample sales, pre-loved wedding dresses and off-the-peg high street ranges all cost considerably less than a made-to-order gown. See EventFlow's second-hand wedding items guide for buying and selling pre-loved dresses safely.

Mother of the Bride Outfits

The mother of the bride (and mother of the groom) traditionally dresses slightly more formally than a general guest, without competing with the bride's dress. A few conventions help narrow the search:

  • Avoid white, ivory and cream — the same guest rule applies, doubly so for close family.
  • Coordinate loosely, not identically — the two mothers (or a mother and stepmother) often agree a colour palette in advance to avoid clashing in photos, without needing to match exactly.
  • Dress to the formality of the day — a knee-length or midi dress with a jacket or wrap suits most semi-formal weddings; a full-length gown suits black tie.
  • Comfort over trend — the mother of the bride is usually on her feet and in photos for the entire day, so fit and comfort matter as much as style.

Bridesmaid Dresses

Bridesmaid dresses are usually chosen by the bride (colour, fabric, sometimes an exact style) with some flexibility left to each bridesmaid on the specific cut, so that a range of body shapes feel comfortable in the same palette.

Who pays for the bridesmaid dress?

There's no fixed UK convention. In practice it varies: some brides cover the dress as a thank-you gift, some ask bridesmaids to buy their own, and many split the cost of the dress from alterations, shoes and accessories. The one thing that consistently causes friction is not discussing it early — raise cost expectations as soon as dresses are chosen, not after they've been ordered.

Shoes, Bouquets & Accessories

Bridal shoes

Comfort matters more than height for most brides, given how many hours are spent standing, walking and dancing. Popular choices include block heels or wedges (more stable on grass or cobbles), classic pumps, and a lower "second pair" for the evening reception once feet start to tire.

Bouquet styles

Common bouquet shapes include the round posy (classic, compact), the cascading bouquet (trailing stems, more formal), the hand-tied bouquet (natural, loosely gathered) and the single-stem bouquet (minimalist). As a rough guide, a bridal bouquet typically uses 15–25 stems depending on flower size and fullness, with bridesmaid bouquets slightly smaller.

Common questions

What should you not wear as a wedding guest?

Avoid white, ivory or cream, which are reserved for the bride, and be cautious with all-black at a religious or more traditional ceremony. Beyond that, follow whatever dress code the invitation states.

How do I choose a wedding dress silhouette?

Start from body shape and venue formality rather than trends: A-line suits most shapes and settings, ball gowns suit grander venues, sheath/column dresses suit relaxed or beach settings, and mermaid/fishtail is a more fitted, formal option. Trying on several silhouettes in person is the fastest way to find what actually suits you.

Who pays for bridesmaid dresses?

There's no fixed UK rule — some brides cover the dress as a thank-you gift, some bridesmaids buy their own, and many split the cost between the dress and extras like alterations and shoes. Agreeing this early avoids awkwardness later.
